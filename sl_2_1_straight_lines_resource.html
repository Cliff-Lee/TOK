2. General form

\[ ax + by + d = 0 \]

Best when: you want all terms on one side, or you are comparing equations algebraically.

If \(b\neq0\), then you can rearrange to find the gradient: \(m=-\frac{a}{b}\).

3. Point-gradient form

\[ y-y_1 = m(x-x_1) \]

Best when: you know one point on the line and the gradient.

This form is especially natural after finding gradient from two points.

How to convert between forms

  1. From \(y=mx+c\) to general form: move everything to one side.
  2. From general form to \(y=mx+c\): make \(y\) the subject.
  3. From point-gradient to \(y=mx+c\): expand the bracket and simplify.
  4. From points to equation: find the gradient first, then use point-gradient or \(y=mx+c\).
It often helps to move through point-gradient form first, because it uses the information “a point + a slope” directly.

Parallel lines

\[ m_1 = m_2 \]

If two lines are parallel, they have the same gradient but different intercepts (unless they are the same line).

Perpendicular lines

\[ m_1m_2=-1 \]

If the lines are not vertical/horizontal, the gradients are negative reciprocals of each other.

Real context: mountain road

Question. A road rises \(180\) m over a horizontal distance of \(420\) m. Find the gradient and write an equation if the lower point is \((0,0)\).

\[ m=\frac{180}{420}=\frac{3}{7}\approx0.429 \] So the line can be modelled by \[ y=\frac{3}{7}x \] if the start point is the origin.

Real context: access ramp

Question. The ramp rises \(1.2\) m over a horizontal distance of \(6\) m. Find the gradient. If the entrance is at \((2,0)\), write a point-gradient equation for the ramp.

\[ m=\frac{1.2}{6}=0.2=\frac{1}{5} \] Through \((2,0)\), the point-gradient form is \[ y-0=\frac{1}{5}(x-2) \] which simplifies to \[ y=\frac{1}{5}x-\frac{2}{5}. \]

Suggested lesson flow

  1. Start graphically with \(y=mx+c\). Let students notice the effect of \(m\) and \(c\).
  2. Move to two-point exploration. Use the picture to introduce gradient and intercepts.
  3. Firm up the algebra by naming and comparing the three forms.
  4. Use conversion examples to show that the forms are equivalent views of the same line.
  5. Only then shift into parallel / perpendicular line problems and real contexts.
  6. Finish with the question generator for deliberate practice.

Misconceptions to watch

MisconceptionIntervention
Confusing \(c\) with the \(x\)-intercept.Keep linking \(c\) to where \(x=0\) on the graph.
Thinking steeper always means larger positive gradient.Compare \(-4\) and \(-1\) to discuss magnitude versus sign.
Forgetting that perpendicular gradients are negative reciprocals.Use easy pairs like \(2\) and \(-\frac12\).
Missing the special case of vertical lines.Explicitly discuss \(x=k\) and undefined gradient.
