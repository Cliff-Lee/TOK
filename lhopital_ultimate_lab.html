Part 1

Start with a mystery: why does substitution fail?

Try direct substitution first. When both numerator and denominator go to zero, the expression has not told us the answer; it has only told us that the obvious method has failed.

\[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x}\quad \text{gives}\quad \frac{0}{0}\]

The key idea is local behaviour. Near the point, the numerator and denominator are both changing. Which one changes faster?

Big idea: L’Hôpital’s rule compares the local rates of change of the two functions, not the derivatives of a quotient.

Part 2

Classify before you calculate

L’Hôpital’s rule is powerful because it has boundaries. The first classroom habit should be: substitute or reason about the top and bottom separately, then classify the form.

Allowed immediately

\[\frac{0}{0}\qquad \frac{\infty}{\infty}\qquad \frac{-\infty}{\infty}\]

These quotient forms are the main gateway to the rule.

Transform first

\[0\cdot\infty,\quad \infty-\infty,\quad 1^\infty,\quad 0^0,\quad \infty^0\]

Rewrite as a quotient, or use logarithms for power forms.

Not indeterminate

\[\frac{1}{0},\quad \frac{5}{\infty},\quad \frac{\infty}{2}\]

Do not use the rule automatically. The limit may be infinite, zero, one-sided, or not exist.

Quick classifier

Choose the form. The goal is speed and accuracy before doing any differentiation.

Part 3

The rule, stated carefully

L’Hôpital’s rule: Suppose \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable near \(a\), and \(g'(x)\neq 0\) near \(a\). If the original quotient has the form \(0/0\) or \(\pm\infty/\pm\infty\), then

\[\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}\]

provided the limit on the right exists, including the possibility that it is infinite.

Do not say: “Differentiate the fraction.” Say: “Differentiate the numerator and denominator separately.”

The six-step exam routine

  1. Substitute / reason about numerator and denominator separately.
  2. Check the form: only use the rule for \(0/0\) or \(\infty/\infty\).
  3. Differentiate separately: \(f'\) over \(g'\), not the quotient rule.
  4. Simplify before substituting again.
  5. Repeat only if the new quotient is still indeterminate.
  6. Interpret: finite answer, infinite limit, one-sided issue, or no limit.

Part 4

Proof lab: why the rule makes sense

This proof is for the \(0/0\) case and uses the derivative-from-first-principles idea. Reveal each step only after predicting what comes next.

Step 1 — Start with the failed quotient
\[\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)},\quad f(a)=0,\quad g(a)=0\]

Because both functions approach zero, direct substitution gives \(0/0\).

Step 2 — Insert the zero values
\[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{g(x)-g(a)}\]

This is legal because \(f(a)=g(a)=0\).

Step 3 — Create difference quotients
\[\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{g(x)-g(a)}=\frac{\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}}{\frac{g(x)-g(a)}{x-a}}\]

Dividing top and bottom by \(x-a\) reveals the derivative structure.

Step 4 — Take the limit
\[\lim_{x\to a}\frac{\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}}{\frac{g(x)-g(a)}{x-a}}=\frac{f'(a)}{g'(a)}\]

The top tends to \(f'(a)\) and the bottom tends to \(g'(a)\), provided \(g'(a)\neq0\).

Step 5 — Generalise the idea
\[\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}\]

The more rigorous theorem handles cases where the derivative quotient has its own limit near \(a\), not just exactly at \(a\).

Important rigour note

This classroom proof captures the intuition. A fully rigorous proof uses stronger results about limits, differentiability, and usually Cauchy’s mean value theorem.

Teacher notes

Suggested teaching sequence

Lesson 1: discovery to rule

  1. Start with \(\sin x/x\) and table/graph behaviour.
  2. Ask students to compare \(f/g\) with \(f'/g'\).
  3. Name indeterminate forms and non-examples.
  4. State the rule only after students have seen the need for it.

Lesson 2: fluency and judgement

  1. Use the proof lab as a structured explanation.
  2. Practise \(0/0\), \(\infty/\infty\), repeated use, and transformations.
  3. Use the mistake cards for mini-whiteboard diagnosis.
  4. Finish with exam-style mixed problems.
Assessment language: Encourage students to write “This is of the form \(0/0\), so L’Hôpital’s rule may be used” before differentiating.
