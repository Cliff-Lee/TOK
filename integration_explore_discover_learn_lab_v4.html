SL 5.5 Integration

The central question

How can we find a total amount when we only know a changing rate?

Integration is the mathematics of accumulation. It can mean area, distance, volume, total cost, total change, or any quantity built up from many small pieces.

Journey through the lab: first estimate messy quantities, then approximate area with rectangles and trapezoids, then discover why anti-differentiation gives exact answers.

Three meanings you must connect

Area

The definite integral can represent the area between a positive curve and the x-axis.

Accumulation

Area under a rate graph gives a total amount, such as distance from a velocity-time graph.

Anti-derivative

Integration reverses differentiation, but the constant of integration must be handled carefully.

Success criteria

By the end, you should be able to:

  • explain what an integral is accumulating;
  • estimate areas using rectangles and trapezoids;
  • integrate powers of x, including negative integer powers except x⁻¹;
  • use a boundary condition to find +C;
  • write the correct definite integral before using technology;
  • answer exam-style questions with clear working.
Key exam habit: write the integral first, then calculate.

Why does this lead to integration?

Each strip is a small area. Adding all strips gives an approximate total area. As the strips become thinner, the approximation approaches the definite integral.

Area ≈ sum of small pieces → exact area = ∫ab f(x) dx

SL 5.8: Approximating areas using the trapezoidal rule

The trapezoidal rule estimates an area by replacing curved or irregular pieces with trapezoids.

Syllabus meaning: given a table of data or a function, estimate the area using trapezoids with equal-width intervals.

The rule

If the x-values are equally spaced, the width is h.

Area ≈ h/2[first y + last y + 2 × sum of the middle y-values]
Symbol version
Area ≈ h/2 [ y₀ + yₙ + 2(y₁ + y₂ + ... + yₙ₋₁) ]

Why trapezoids?

Rectangles use a flat top. Trapezoids use a sloping top, so they usually follow the curve more closely. This is especially useful for:

irregular areas such as lakes velocity-time graphs experimental data functions where exact integration is not required
Exam method
1
Check equal width.
The x-values must increase by the same amount each time.
2
Find h.
This is the gap between consecutive x-values.
3
Substitute carefully.
First and last y-values are used once. Middle y-values are doubled.
4
Interpret the answer.
If the graph is velocity-time, the area estimates distance. If it is an irregular region, the answer estimates area.

IdeaSL 5.5SL 5.8
Area under a curve Use definite integrals, often with technology, when the function is known. Use trapezoids to estimate the area, especially from a table or measured data.
Approximation Rectangles help develop the idea of limiting area. Trapezoids are the required numerical approximation method.
Real-world context Rates accumulate to totals; positive curves give areas. Irregular regions, lakes, maps, and kinematics can be estimated without exact formulae.
Technology Graphing software can calculate exact/numerical definite integrals. Dynamic graphing software can show trapezoids and help interpret the estimate.
Enrichment: Simpson’s rule is another numerical integration technique. It uses parabolic arcs instead of straight-line trapezoid tops.

Learn: anti-differentiation

To integrate a power of x, increase the power by 1 and divide by the new power.

∫ axⁿ dx = a · xⁿ⁺¹ / (n+1) + C,   where n ≠ -1
Why +C? Many functions can have the same derivative. For example, x² + 1, x² + 7, and x² − 100 all have derivative \(2x\).

Definite integrals and area

A definite integral has limits. For a positive curve, it gives the area between the curve and the x-axis.

1
Write the expression
∫ab f(x) dx
2
Find an anti-derivative
F′(x) = f(x)
3
Subtract
F(b) − F(a)

Common mistakes

MistakeWhy it mattersFix
Forgetting +CIndefinite integrals describe a family of functions.Always add + C unless limits are given.
Using the old power after integrationThe power must increase by 1.xn → xn+1 ÷ (n+1).
Not writing limitsA definite integral needs a start and end value.Write ∫ab, not just ∫.
Calling signed area “area” when graph is below x-axisArea is positive; definite integrals can be negative.For SL 5.5, focus on f(x) > 0, or split if needed.

Exit ticket

  1. In one sentence, explain what an integral accumulates.
  2. Why do thinner rectangles give a better estimate?
  3. Find ∫(4x³ − 2x + 5) dx.
  4. Write, but do not calculate, the area under \(y=3x^2+4\) from \(x=2\) to \(x=6\).
  5. What does +C mean?
Possible answers
  1. It accumulates small changes or small areas to find a total.
  2. The rectangles follow the curve more closely.
  3. \(x^4-x^2+5x+C\).
  4. ∫26(3x² + 4) dx.
  5. A family of vertical shifts with the same derivative.
