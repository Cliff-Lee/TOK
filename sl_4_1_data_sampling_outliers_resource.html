Discrete and continuous data

\[ \text{Discrete: counted values} \qquad \text{Continuous: measured values} \]

Discrete data usually comes from counting. Continuous data usually comes from measuring, and can in principle take any value in an interval.

Discrete

Number of siblings, goals scored, books borrowed, students in a class.

Continuous

Height, time, temperature, mass, distance, speed.

Context matters. “Age in years” may be recorded as discrete whole numbers, even though time itself is continuous.

Sampling techniques

Simple random

Every member has an equal chance of being selected. Often needs a complete list and a random number generator.

Convenience

Choose whoever is easiest to reach. Quick, but often biased.

Systematic

Choose every \(k\)th member after a random start. Efficient, but patterns in the list can cause bias.

Quota

Fill fixed numbers from different groups, but selection inside groups may not be random.

Stratified

Split population into strata, then randomly sample from each in proportion to the population.

Reliability of data sources

Before analysing the data, ask whether the source is trustworthy and relevant.

  1. Who collected the data?
  2. Why was the data collected?
  3. How was the sample chosen?
  4. How large was the sample?
  5. Who or what might be missing?
  6. Could people have misunderstood, lied, or responded selectively?
A mathematically correct calculation can still support a misleading conclusion if the data source is poor.

Missing data and recording errors

Missing data is not just an inconvenience. It can change the conclusion, especially if it is not missing randomly.

Missing completely at random

Data missing for reasons unrelated to the value.

Systematic missingness

A certain group is more likely to be missing.

Recording error

A value was typed or measured incorrectly.

Impossible value

A value violates context, such as negative height.

Do not automatically delete unusual values. First ask whether they are errors or valid extreme cases.

Definition

\[ \mathrm{IQR}=Q_3-Q_1 \] \[ \text{lower fence}=Q_1-1.5\mathrm{IQR} \] \[ \text{upper fence}=Q_3+1.5\mathrm{IQR} \]

A value is an outlier if it is below the lower fence or above the upper fence.

The rule identifies unusual values. It does not automatically decide whether the value is wrong.
In context, an outlier might be a valid part of the sample, or it might be a recording error.

TOK and Aim 8 prompts

TOK: How easy is it to be misled by statistics?

Statistics can mislead through biased samples, selective reporting, misleading graphs, poor definitions, missing data, and confusing correlation with causation. The mathematics may be correct while the conclusion is still unreliable.

Aim 8: Is it ever justifiable to purposely use statistics to mislead others?

This raises ethical questions about persuasion, public policy, advertising, medical decisions, and trust. A useful distinction is between simplifying for communication and distorting evidence to manipulate decisions.

Formal definitions

TermMeaning
PopulationThe whole group of individuals or items being studied.
SampleA subset of the population from which data is collected.
Random sampleA sample chosen using chance, ideally so each member has a known chance of selection.
Discrete dataData that takes countable separate values.
Continuous dataData that is measured and can take any value in an interval.
BiasA systematic distortion in the way data is collected, recorded, or interpreted.

Worked examples

Example 1. A school wants to know the average sleep time of all Grade 11 students. A teacher asks the first 20 students entering the library.

The population is all Grade 11 students. The sample is the 20 students entering the library. This is convenience sampling, not a random sample. It may be biased if library users have different routines from other students.

Example 2. A data set has \(Q_1=18\), \(Q_3=30\). Find the outlier fences.

\[ \mathrm{IQR}=30-18=12 \] \[ 1.5\times \mathrm{IQR}=1.5\times12=18 \] Lower fence: \[ 18-18=0 \] Upper fence: \[ 30+18=48 \] Values below 0 or above 48 are outliers.

Example 3. A phone app collects reports of potholes. What might be biased?

The app may overrepresent people with smartphones, people who know about the app, and neighbourhoods where users are more likely to report. Areas with fewer app users may appear to have fewer potholes even if they do not.

Data-analysis question checklist

Population: who or what is the claim about?
Sample: who or what was actually measured?
Method: how were sample members chosen?
Variables: are they discrete or continuous?
Quality: are there missing values or recording errors?
Outliers: are they valid extremes or mistakes?
Bias: who is overrepresented or missing?
Conclusion: does the claim go beyond the evidence?

Suggested lesson flow

  1. Begin with the sampling simulator: show that different samples from the same population can produce different conclusions.
  2. Clarify population, sample, random sample, and representativeness.
  3. Sort variables into discrete and continuous, stressing context.
  4. Compare sampling techniques and ask when each might be effective or biased.
  5. Use the missing/error tab before the outlier rule so students do not treat data cleaning as mechanical.
  6. Introduce the \(1.5\times\mathrm{IQR}\) rule, then discuss whether outliers are valid or suspicious.
  7. Finish with famous misleading-statistics examples and exam-style practice.

Misconceptions to watch

MisconceptionIntervention
“Large sample” automatically means “good sample”.Use biased sample examples to show representativeness matters.
Random means “whatever happens”.Stress a deliberate chance method such as random numbers.
Outlier means wrong.Separate statistical unusualness from contextual error.
Delete missing data without comment.Ask whether missingness is random or systematic.
Confusing stratified and quota.Stratified uses random samples within strata; quota fills target numbers but may not be random.
