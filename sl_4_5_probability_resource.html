Theoretical probability

\[ P(A)=\frac{n(A)}{n(U)} \]

This formula applies when the outcomes in the sample space are equally likely.

Example. Roll a fair die. Let \(A\) be the event “roll a prime number”.

The sample space is \(U=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\), so \(n(U)=6\). Prime outcomes are \(A=\{2,3,5\}\), so \(n(A)=3\). \[ P(A)=\frac{3}{6}=\frac12. \]

Relative frequency simulation

Relative frequency is the experimental probability after a number of trials.

\[ \text{relative frequency}=\frac{\text{number of times event occurs}}{\text{number of trials}} \]

Complementary events

\[ P(A')=1-P(A) \]

The complement \(A'\) means “not \(A\)”. Together, \(A\) and \(A'\) cover the whole sample space.

Choose an event.

Complement shortcut

Question. A spinner has 20 equal sectors. 3 sectors are red. What is the probability it is not red?

\[ P(\text{red})=\frac{3}{20} \] Therefore \[ P(\text{not red})=1-\frac{3}{20}=\frac{17}{20}. \]
Complements are useful when “not happening” is easier to count than “happening”.

Expected number of occurrences

\[ \text{expected number}=n\times p \]

If an event has probability \(p\) and there are \(n\) trials or individuals, the expected number of occurrences is \(np\).

Enter values and calculate.
Expected number does not have to be a whole number. It is an average over many repeated situations.

Expected value intuition

In one particular class, you cannot have 12.8 absent students. But across many days/classes, the average may be about 12.8.

Ethics, gambling and risk perception

Aim 8: What is ethically questionable about gambling products based on probability?

Casinos and betting companies may use mathematically negative expected value games while exploiting emotion, near-misses, addiction, and misunderstanding of probability. The ethical issue is not just that people lose money, but that the design may manipulate risk perception.

TOK: What role does emotion play in our perception of risk?

People may overestimate dramatic, memorable risks and underestimate familiar or slow-moving risks. Theoretical probability gives a model, but emotion affects attention, trust and action.

International-mindedness: St Petersburg paradox

The St Petersburg paradox is a famous problem about expected value and how people actually value risky games. It shows that mathematical expectation and human decision-making are not always the same thing.

Core formulae

\[ P(A)=\frac{n(A)}{n(U)} \] \[ P(A')=1-P(A) \] \[ \text{relative frequency}=\frac{\text{event frequency}}{\text{number of trials}} \] \[ \text{expected number}=np \]
The formula \(P(A)=n(A)/n(U)\) assumes equally likely outcomes.

Worked examples

Example 1. Two fair dice are rolled. Find \(P(\text{sum is }7)\).

There are \(36\) equally likely ordered outcomes. The successful outcomes are \((1,6),(2,5),(3,4),(4,3),(5,2),(6,1)\), so \(n(A)=6\). \[ P(A)=\frac{6}{36}=\frac16. \]

Example 2. \(P(A)=0.23\). Find \(P(A')\).

\[ P(A')=1-P(A)=1-0.23=0.77. \]

Example 3. A machine has probability \(0.04\) of producing a faulty item. In 500 items, how many faulty items are expected?

\[ \text{expected number}=np=500(0.04)=20. \] About 20 faulty items are expected.

Exam checklist

Define \(U\): what are all possible outcomes?
Define \(A\): what outcomes count as success?
Check equally likely: can you use \(n(A)/n(U)\)?
Use complements: \(P(A')=1-P(A)\).
Relative frequency: experimental successes divided by trials.
Expected number: multiply \(n\) by \(p\), and interpret in context.

Suggested lesson flow

  1. Begin with the experimental simulator and let students see noisy relative frequency.
  2. Introduce vocabulary using one familiar experiment such as a die roll.
  3. Build sample spaces as lists and tables, especially for two dice.
  4. Formalise \(P(A)=n(A)/n(U)\) only after students understand equally likely outcomes.
  5. Use complements as an efficient counting strategy.
  6. Introduce expected number as \(np\), stressing that it can be non-integer.
  7. Finish with simulations, contexts, ethics of gambling and risk-perception discussion.

\(A'\) means inverse or reciprocal.Stress complement: “not \(A\)”.
