Explorer 1

Estimation: useful, but not magic

How tall is the stack of boxes?

You rarely begin with perfect data. Estimation means using a sensible reference, making assumptions, and checking whether your answer is plausible.

Choose a reference.
For example, compare the boxes with a student height, a door height, or a tape measure on the floor.
State your assumptions.
Are all boxes the same height? Is perspective making the stack look taller or shorter?
Give a sensible range.
An estimate like “about 2.6 m to 3.0 m” is often more honest than “2.743 m”.
Key teaching point: Estimation is not “guessing badly”. It is structured reasoning when exact measurement is unavailable. Ask students to defend their assumptions before calculating.
Mini task

Quick estimate

A stack is a little taller than a student of height 1.68 m. It looks about 1.6 times the student’s height.

Estimate the height of the stack.

1.6 × 1.68 = 2.688, so a sensible estimate is about 2.7 m. Since the multiplier was visual, reporting 2.688 m would be false precision.
Discussion

When can accuracy be misleading?

Which answer sounds more trustworthy: about 2.7 m or 2.688 m? Why?

Good answers should mention data quality, measurement limits, and whether extra decimal places have real meaning.

Core skill

Significant figures: the digits that carry information

A measurement such as 12.457 g tells us more than just “about 12 g”. It suggests the balance reports to the nearest 0.001 g. Significant figures are the meaningful digits in a number.

Measurement idea: A measuring instrument cannot give unlimited accuracy. The last displayed digit is usually the least certain digit.
RuleExample
Non-zero digits are significant.47.3 has 3 s.f.
Zeros between non-zero digits are significant.1009 has 4 s.f.
Leading zeros are not significant.0.0048 has 2 s.f.
Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant.3.400 has 4 s.f.
Trailing zeros in whole numbers can be ambiguous unless written in scientific notation.1200 may mean 2, 3 or 4 s.f.; 1.20 × 10³ means 3 s.f.

Significant figure checker

Try 0.00340, 1009, 1200, or 1.20e3.

Why zeros matter

3.4 g and 3.400 g may have the same value, but they do not communicate the same precision.

3.4 g = measured to about tenths
3.400 g = measured to about thousandths
Core skill

Rounding: report the answer honestly

Rounding is not just tidying. It is choosing a number of digits that matches the quality of the data and the purpose of the answer.

Decimal places

Decimal places count digits after the decimal point.

8.7362 to 2 d.p. = 8.74

Significant figures

Significant figures count meaningful digits from the first non-zero digit.

0.0087362 to 2 s.f. = 0.0087

Exam rule of thumb

Unless instructed otherwise, final answers are often exact or rounded to 3 significant figures. Avoid rounding intermediate values.

Store full calculator values.
Round only the final answer.

Exam skill

Upper and lower bounds

If a carpenter measures a shelf as 84.6 cm to the nearest 0.1 cm, the exact length is not necessarily 84.6 cm. It could be slightly less or slightly more.

If a value is recorded as M to the nearest u, then

M − 0.5u ≤ exact value < M + 0.5u

Example

84.6 cm to the nearest 0.1 cm gives:

84.55 cm ≤ L < 84.65 cm

84.65 would round up to 84.7, not 84.6. That is why we write < for the upper bound.

Bounds machine

Language check

“Measured to the nearest 0.1 cm” means the exact value lies within half of 0.1 cm.

Common error: using ±0.1 instead of ±0.05.

Compound bounds

When measurements combine, errors combine too

Bounds become more powerful when a calculation uses several measured values. For area, volume, density, speed, cost, or BMI, the maximum and minimum are found by choosing the right combination of bounds.

Principle: To maximise a positive product, use the upper bounds. To minimise it, use the lower bounds. For subtraction, think carefully about which part should be large or small.

Garden example

A rectangular garden is measured as 12.4 m by 7.8 m, both to the nearest 0.1 m.

Length bounds: 12.35 ≤ L < 12.45
Width bounds: 7.75 ≤ W < 7.85

Minimum rectangle area ≈ 12.35 × 7.75 = 95.71 m²
Maximum rectangle area ≈ 12.45 × 7.85 = 97.73 m²

Exam skill

Percentage error: how serious is the error?

An absolute error of 0.2 seconds is huge in a sprint but almost irrelevant in a two-hour journey. Percentage error compares the error with the exact or accepted value.

percentage error = |approximate − exact| ÷ exact × 100%

Race timing example

Official time = 10.98 s, measured time = 11.23 s.

Absolute error = |11.23 − 10.98| = 0.25 s

Percentage error = 0.25 ÷ 10.98 × 100% ≈ 2.28%

Final summary

The whole topic in one page

1. Every measurement has limits

A number from a measuring tool is not infinitely accurate. Ask: nearest what?

2. Rounding communicates precision

Use decimal places or significant figures to report an answer honestly.

3. Bounds tell the possible range

If M is measured to the nearest u, then M − 0.5u ≤ exact value < M + 0.5u.

4. Percentage error compares fairly

Divide the absolute error by the exact value, then multiply by 100%.

5. Context decides accuracy

Currency, engineering, sport timing and science experiments need different levels of precision.

6. Estimation catches nonsense

Before trusting a calculator result, check size, units and whether the answer could be possible.
