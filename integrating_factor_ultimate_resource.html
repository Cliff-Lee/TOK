Integrating Factors

A guided resource for discovering, understanding, applying, and practising the method for first-order linear differential equations.

Target form: \(y' + P(x)y = Q(x)\) Goal: make the left side a product derivative Method: \(\mu(x)=e^{\int P(x)\,dx}\)
Overview 1. Discovery 2. Worked example 3. Derivation 4. Method lab 5. Modelling 6. Exam practice Teacher notes

What this topic is really about

The surface skill

Solve equations in the form

\[\frac{dy}{dx}+P(x)y=Q(x).\]

Students need to put the equation into standard form, find the integrating factor, multiply through, integrate, and use any initial condition.

The deeper idea

The left side is not usually a derivative of one simple expression. The integrating factor is a multiplier that forces it to become one:

\[\mu y' + \mu P y = \frac{d}{dx}(\mu y).\]
Learning route: first discover the missing product rule, then work one clean example, then prove the formula, then apply it in modelling and exam questions.

1. Discovery: what multiplier would make this a product rule?

Begin with a simple equation:

\[\frac{dy}{dx}+\frac{2}{x}y=x^3 \qquad (x>0).\]

Try multiplying the whole equation by one of the expressions below. Which one makes the left-hand side become a perfect product-rule derivative?

Choose a multiplier and check whether the left side matches the derivative of something like \(\mu(x)y\).

Product-rule target

If \(\mu=\mu(x)\), then

\[\frac{d}{dx}(\mu y)=\mu\frac{dy}{dx}+\mu' y.\]

So after multiplying by \(\mu\), the coefficient of \(y\) must be \(\mu'\).

Compare with the equation

Multiplying \(y'+P(x)y=Q(x)\) by \(\mu\) gives

\[\mu y' + \mu P(x)y=\mu Q(x).\]

For this to match the product rule, we need \(\mu P(x)=\mu'\).

2. Worked example: one clean exam-style solution

Solve the differential equation

\[\frac{dy}{dx}+\frac{2}{x}y=x^3, \qquad x>0.\]

Identify \(P(x)\) and \(Q(x)\).

\[P(x)=\frac{2}{x},\qquad Q(x)=x^3.\]

Find the integrating factor.

\[\mu(x)=e^{\int P(x)\,dx}=e^{\int 2/x\,dx}=e^{2\ln x}=x^2.\]

Multiply the whole equation by \(x^2\).

\[x^2\frac{dy}{dx}+2xy=x^5.\]

Recognise the product-rule derivative.

\[\frac{d}{dx}(x^2y)=x^5.\]

That is the key moment: the awkward left-hand side has become one derivative.

Integrate and solve for \(y\).

\[x^2y=\int x^5\,dx=\frac{x^6}{6}+C\]
\[\boxed{y=\frac{x^4}{6}+\frac{C}{x^2}}.\]
Exam habit: if the equation is not already in the form \(y'+P(x)y=Q(x)\), divide through by the coefficient of \(y'\) first.

3. Derivation: where does \(e^{\int P(x)\,dx}\) come from?

The method is not magic. It is just the product rule used backwards.

Start with the standard form

\[y'+P(x)y=Q(x).\]

Multiply through by an unknown function \(\mu(x)\):

\[\mu y' + \mu P(x)y=\mu Q(x).\]

Force a product rule

We want the left-hand side to be

\[\frac{d}{dx}(\mu y)=\mu y'+\mu' y.\]

So we require

\[\mu P(x)=\mu'.\]

From \(\mu P(x)=\mu'\), assuming \(\mu\ne0\),

\[\frac{\mu'}{\mu}=P(x).\]

Integrate both sides:

\[\int \frac{\mu'}{\mu}\,dx=\int P(x)\,dx.\]
\[\ln|\mu|=\int P(x)\,dx+C.\]

Exponentiate:

\[\mu=Ae^{\int P(x)\,dx}.\]

The constant multiplier \(A\) is not needed because multiplying the whole equation by \(A\mu\) instead of \(\mu\) gives the same family of solutions. So we take

\[\boxed{\mu(x)=e^{\int P(x)\,dx}}.\]
The entire method in one line: \[ y'+P(x)y=Q(x) \quad \xrightarrow{\times\, e^{\int Pdx}} \quad \frac{d}{dx}\left(e^{\int Pdx}y\right)=e^{\int Pdx}Q(x). \]

4. Method lab: practise recognising the structure

Choose a case. For each one, the essential question is: what is \(P(x)\), and what integrating factor does it produce?

Select a case to display the solution structure.

Reliable checklist

  1. Put the equation into \(y'+P(x)y=Q(x)\).
  2. Identify \(P(x)\), not the whole left side.
  3. Compute \(\mu=e^{\int P(x)dx}\). Do not add a constant of integration here.
  4. Multiply every term by \(\mu\).
  5. Rewrite the left side as \(\frac{d}{dx}(\mu y)\).
  6. Integrate both sides.
  7. Divide by \(\mu\), then use any initial condition.

Question 1

Solve \(\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dx}+4y=8\).

Here \(P(x)=4\), so \(\mu=e^{\int4dx}=e^{4x}\).

\[e^{4x}y'+4e^{4x}y=8e^{4x}\]
\[\frac{d}{dx}(e^{4x}y)=8e^{4x}\]
\[e^{4x}y=2e^{4x}+C\]
\[\boxed{y=2+Ce^{-4x}}.\]
Variable coefficient

Question 2

Solve \(\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dx}+\frac{3}{x}y=x^2\), where \(x>0\).

\(P(x)=3/x\), so \(\mu=e^{\int3/x\,dx}=e^{3\ln x}=x^3\).

\[x^3y'+3x^2y=x^5\]
\[\frac{d}{dx}(x^3y)=x^5\]
\[x^3y=\frac{x^6}{6}+C\]
\[\boxed{y=\frac{x^3}{6}+\frac{C}{x^3}}.\]
Standard form first

Question 3

Solve \(\displaystyle (x+1)\frac{dy}{dx}-3y=(x+1)^4\), where \(x>-1\).

First divide by \(x+1\):

\[y'-\frac{3}{x+1}y=(x+1)^3.\]

So \(P(x)=-3/(x+1)\), and

\[\mu=e^{\int -3/(x+1)\,dx}=e^{-3\ln(x+1)}=(x+1)^{-3}.\]
\[\frac{d}{dx}\left((x+1)^{-3}y\right)=1.\]
\[(x+1)^{-3}y=x+C\]
\[\boxed{y=(x+1)^3(x+C)}.\]
Initial condition

Question 4

Find the particular solution of \(\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dx}+2xy=2x\), given \(y(0)=5\).

\(P(x)=2x\), so \(\mu=e^{\int2x dx}=e^{x^2}\).

\[\frac{d}{dx}(e^{x^2}y)=2xe^{x^2}.\]

Integrate:

\[e^{x^2}y=e^{x^2}+C.\]
\[y=1+Ce^{-x^2}.\]

Use \(y(0)=5\): \(5=1+C\), so \(C=4\).

\[\boxed{y=1+4e^{-x^2}}.\]
ModellingInterpretation

Question 5

A tank contains 100 L of water. Brine containing 3 kg of salt per litre flows in at 4 L/min and the well-mixed solution flows out at 4 L/min. Initially the tank contains pure water. Let \(A(t)\) be the mass of salt in kg after \(t\) minutes.

  1. Form a differential equation for \(A\).
  2. Solve it.
  3. Find the long-term amount of salt in the tank.

Input rate: \(3\times4=12\) kg/min.

Output concentration: \(A/100\) kg/L, so output rate: \(4(A/100)=A/25\) kg/min.

\[\frac{dA}{dt}=12-\frac{A}{25}.\]
\[A'+\frac{1}{25}A=12.\]

Integrating factor: \(\mu=e^{t/25}\).

\[\frac{d}{dt}(e^{t/25}A)=12e^{t/25}.\]
\[e^{t/25}A=300e^{t/25}+C\]
\[A=300+Ce^{-t/25}.\]

Since \(A(0)=0\), \(0=300+C\), so \(C=-300\).

\[\boxed{A(t)=300(1-e^{-t/25})}.\]

Long-term amount: \(\boxed{300\text{ kg}}\), which matches 100 L at 3 kg/L.

HarderForcing term

Question 6

Solve \(\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dx}-2y=e^x\), given \(y(0)=3\).

\(P(x)=-2\), so \(\mu=e^{-2x}\).

\[\frac{d}{dx}(e^{-2x}y)=e^{-2x}e^x=e^{-x}.\]
\[e^{-2x}y=-e^{-x}+C.\]
\[y=-e^x+Ce^{2x}.\]

Use \(y(0)=3\): \(3=-1+C\), so \(C=4\).

\[\boxed{y=-e^x+4e^{2x}}.\]

Teacher notes: suggested lesson sequence

60–75 minute lesson

  1. 0–10 min: product-rule warm-up. Ask: “What derivative gives \(x^2y'+2xy\)?”
  2. 10–25 min: discovery multiplier activity.
  3. 25–40 min: worked example, slowly, with students predicting each next line.
  4. 40–50 min: derivation of \(\mu=e^{\int Pdx}\).
  5. 50–65 min: method lab cases.
  6. 65–75 min: exam practice Q1–Q3.

Common misconceptions

  • Forgetting to put the equation into standard form first.
  • Using \(Q(x)\) instead of \(P(x)\) to find the integrating factor.
  • Adding a constant when calculating the integrating factor.
  • Multiplying only some terms by \(\mu\).
  • Not recognising \(\mu y'+\mu' y\) as \(\frac{d}{dx}(\mu y)\).
Best assessment question type: give one equation not in standard form, one with an initial condition, and one modelling problem where students must form the differential equation before solving it.
