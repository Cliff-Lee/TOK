Why study coupled linear differential equations?

Many real systems have two quantities that change together. The key idea is that the rate of change of one quantity can depend on the other quantity. In this applet, \(x\) and \(y\) often mean deviation from a normal or balanced level, so \((0,0)\) means “back to balance”.

\[\begin{cases}x'=ax+by\\y'=cx+dy\end{cases}\qquad\text{or}\qquad \mathbf{x}'=M\mathbf{x}\]
Mechanical motion

A damped spring: position and velocity

If \(x\) is position and \(y\) is velocity, then position changes because of velocity, and velocity changes because of the spring force and friction.

\[x'=y,\qquad y'=-2x-0.4y\]
Ecology

Predator–prey changes near balance

Near an equilibrium, small changes in prey and predator populations can be approximated by a linear system. One variable pushes the other.

\[x'=-2y,\qquad y'=x\]
Mixing and diffusion

Two connected tanks or rooms

Salt, heat, smoke, or pollution can move between two connected places. If \(x\) and \(y\) are deviations from normal concentration, the system is coupled.

\[x'=-0.5x+0.2y,\qquad y'=0.3x-0.4y\]
Economics and behaviour

Two linked quantities adjusting together

For example, price pressure and demand pressure may affect each other. A linear model can describe small movements around a steady state.

\[x'=-0.6x+0.8y,\qquad y'=0.2x-0.5y\]
Electric circuits

An RLC circuit: charge and current

Charge changes because of current. Current changes because of resistance, capacitance, and inductance. This produces another coupled linear system.

\[q'=i,\qquad i'=-3q-0.7i\]
Social systems

Two opinions or behaviours influencing each other

A simple model can describe how two attitudes, habits, or groups pull each other towards or away from a balance point.

\[x'=0.4x+0.9y,\qquad y'=0.6x+0.1y\]
Why eigenvalues matter: they tell us the long-term story: return to balance, spiral in, spiral out, move away, or split into saddle behaviour.

What the diagram means

Start from the very basics

1. A normal differential equation has one changing quantity

For example, \(x'=kx\). The rate of change of \(x\) depends only on \(x\).

\[x'=kx \quad\Rightarrow\quad x(t)=Ce^{kt}\]

2. A coupled system has two changing quantities

Now the rate of change of \(x\) can depend on \(x\) and \(y\), and the rate of change of \(y\) can also depend on \(x\) and \(y\).

\[\begin{cases}x'=ax+by\\y'=cx+dy\end{cases}\]

3. Write it as one vector equation

This is cleaner and reveals the linear algebra.

\[\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{x}'=\begin{bmatrix}x'\\y'\end{bmatrix},\qquad M=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}\]
\[\mathbf{x}'=M\mathbf{x}\]

4. The phase plane shows motion

Each point \((x,y)\) has an arrow. The arrow is the velocity vector:

\[\begin{bmatrix}x'\\y'\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}\]

A solution curve is the path followed from a starting point.

The conceptual bridge

The best way to understand the exact solution is to copy the one-variable idea, but with a vector:

\[\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{v}e^{kt}\]

Substitute it into \(\mathbf{x}'=M\mathbf{x}\):

\[k\mathbf{v}e^{kt}=M\mathbf{v}e^{kt}\]

Since \(e^{kt}\neq0\), this becomes

\[M\mathbf{v}=k\mathbf{v}\]

That is exactly the eigenvalue equation. So:

Eigenvectors give straight-line directions of motion. Eigenvalues give the exponential growth or decay rate along those directions.

Exact solution method

For a \(2\times2\) system, set \(m=\frac{a+d}{2}\) and \(p=ad-bc\). Then \(\lambda=m\pm\sqrt{m^2-p}\). For real distinct eigenvalues, the exact solution is built from two eigenvector motions.

\[\mathbf{x}(t)=C_1e^{\lambda_1t}\mathbf{v}_1+C_2e^{\lambda_2t}\mathbf{v}_2\]
Write the system in matrix form: \(\mathbf{x}'=M\mathbf{x}\).
Calculate the shortcut quantities: \(m=\frac{a+d}{2}\) and \(p=ad-bc\).
Find eigenvalues: \(\lambda=m\pm\sqrt{m^2-p}\).
Find eigenvectors: \(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2\).
Write the general solution: combine both exponential eigenvector motions.
Use the initial condition: solve \(\mathbf{x}(0)=C_1\mathbf{v}_1+C_2\mathbf{v}_2\).
Interpret the behaviour: stable, unstable, saddle, centre, or spiral.

How to decide clockwise or anticlockwise

Look at the arrow at a simple point. At \((1,0)\),

\[\begin{bmatrix}x'\\y'\end{bmatrix}=M\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a\\c\end{bmatrix}\]

If \(c>0\), the arrow points upward on the positive \(x\)-axis, so the motion is anticlockwise. If \(c<0\), it is clockwise.
