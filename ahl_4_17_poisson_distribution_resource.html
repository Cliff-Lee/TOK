Poisson models counts: 0 calls, 1 call, 2 calls, and so on. The count is discrete, but the interval can be time, space, length or area.

What to notice

  1. The same average rate does not mean the same count every interval.
  2. Small samples are noisy; many intervals reveal the shape more clearly.
  3. As \(\lambda\) increases, the centre moves right and the shape becomes more symmetric.

The Poisson formula

\[ X\sim \mathrm{Po}(\lambda) \] \[ P(X=x)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!},\qquad x=0,1,2,\ldots \]

\(\lambda\) is the mean number of events in the interval. The random variable \(X\) counts how many events occur.

Example. Calls arrive at an average rate of 4 per minute. Find \(P(X=2)\).

Here \(X\sim\mathrm{Po}(4)\). \[ P(X=2)=\frac{e^{-4}4^2}{2!} =\frac{16e^{-4}}{2} =8e^{-4} \approx 0.1465. \]

When is Poisson appropriate?

  1. The variable is a count of events in a fixed interval.
  2. Events occur independently.
  3. Events occur at a uniform average rate during the period of interest.
  4. The events are not naturally limited by a fixed small number of trials.
Poisson often appears when we count rare-ish events across time, space or text: calls per minute, typos per page, mutations per strand, accidents per day.
If the rate changes during the interval, or events trigger other events, a simple Poisson model may be unreliable.

Mean and variance

\[ X\sim\mathrm{Po}(\lambda) \] \[ E(X)=\lambda \] \[ \mathrm{Var}(X)=\lambda \] \[ \sigma=\sqrt{\lambda} \]

The mean and variance are equal for a Poisson distribution. This is a distinctive feature of the model.

A data set whose sample mean and sample variance are very different may suggest the simple Poisson model is not suitable.

Interpretation

Mean

The expected count in the interval.

Variance

For Poisson, the spread is tied to the mean.

Standard deviation

\(\sqrt{\lambda}\), useful for judging typical variation.

Model check

Observed data may show overdispersion: variance larger than the mean.

Sum of independent Poisson distributions

If two independent counts are Poisson, their sum is also Poisson, with parameter equal to the sum of parameters.

\[ X\sim\mathrm{Po}(\lambda_1),\quad Y\sim\mathrm{Po}(\lambda_2),\quad X\perp Y \] \[ X+Y\sim\mathrm{Po}(\lambda_1+\lambda_2) \]

Context example

Calls to two independent support teams arrive at average rates of 3 and 5 per hour. What is the distribution of the total number of calls per hour?

Let \(X\sim\mathrm{Po}(3)\) and \(Y\sim\mathrm{Po}(5)\). If the two call streams are independent, then \[ X+Y\sim\mathrm{Po}(3+5)=\mathrm{Po}(8). \]
Current result
Independence matters. If one event stream affects the other, the simple sum rule may not apply.

Choose the distribution: normal, binomial or Poisson?

Binomial

Fixed number of independent trials. Two outcomes per trial. Same probability each trial. Counts successes.

Poisson

Counts events in a fixed interval. Independent events. Uniform average rate.

Normal

Continuous measurement. Bell-shaped. Often suitable for naturally varying measurements or approximations.

Decision tree

  1. Is the variable a continuous measurement? Consider normal.
  2. Is it a count of successes out of a fixed number of trials? Consider binomial.
  3. Is it a count of events in a fixed interval with uniform rate? Consider Poisson.
  4. Check assumptions before calculating.

Common traps

Two dice sums

Not Poisson; use sample space or another discrete distribution.

Number defective in 50 items

Usually binomial: fixed number of trials.

Waiting time

The count may be Poisson, but waiting time itself is not Poisson.

Changing rates

Rush hour traffic may not have a uniform average rate across the whole day.

Step-by-step technology guide

  1. Use Poisson Pdf for exactly \(x\): \(P(X=x)\).
  2. Use Poisson Cdf for cumulative probability: \(P(X\le x)\).
  3. For \(P(X\lt x)\), calculate \(P(X\le x-1)\).
  4. For \(P(X\ge x)\), use \(1-P(X\le x-1)\).
  5. For \(P(X>x)\), use \(1-P(X\le x)\).
  6. For an interval \(P(a\le X\le b)\), calculate \(P(X\le b)-P(X\le a-1)\).
Always write the distribution first, for example \(X\sim\mathrm{Po}(4)\), then show the probability statement before using technology.

TOK: trust in models

Prompt. To what extent can mathematical models such as the Poisson distribution be trusted?

A model can be trusted only relative to its assumptions and purpose. A Poisson model may give useful planning estimates even when it is not perfectly true. But if independence or uniform rate fails, the model can become misleading. The TOK issue is not whether the formula is true, but whether the world matches the model closely enough for the intended decision.

Prompt. What role do mathematical models play in other areas of knowledge?

Models simplify reality so that we can reason, predict and decide. In sciences, models help explain and forecast; in economics and politics, they guide policy; in medicine, they estimate risk. But every model highlights some features and ignores others.

Worked examples

Example 1. Typos occur at a mean rate of 1.5 per page. Find the probability of exactly 2 typos on a page.

Let \(X\sim\mathrm{Po}(1.5)\). \[ P(X=2)=\frac{e^{-1.5}1.5^2}{2!}\approx 0.251. \]

Example 2. Emergency admissions occur at a mean rate of 6 per hour. Find \(P(X\ge 8)\).

Let \(X\sim\mathrm{Po}(6)\). \[ P(X\ge8)=1-P(X\le7). \] Using technology, \[ P(X\ge8)\approx 0.256. \]

Example 3. Two independent roads have mean accident counts 0.8 and 1.3 per month. Find the distribution of total accidents.

If \(X\sim\mathrm{Po}(0.8)\), \(Y\sim\mathrm{Po}(1.3)\), and they are independent, then \[ X+Y\sim\mathrm{Po}(0.8+1.3)=\mathrm{Po}(2.1). \]

Poisson modelling checklist

Count variable: \(0,1,2,\ldots\), not a continuous measurement.
Fixed interval: time, area, page, kilometre, machine-hour.
Average rate: identify \(\lambda\) for that exact interval.
Independence: one event does not make another more or less likely.
Uniformity: rate is roughly constant during the interval.
Choose model: compare with binomial and normal before calculating.

Exam checklist

Define \(X\): say exactly what is being counted.
State distribution: \(X\sim\mathrm{Po}(\lambda)\).
Match interval: adjust \(\lambda\) if the time/space interval changes.
Translate wording: exactly, at most, fewer than, at least, more than.
Use complement: \(P(X\ge k)=1-P(X\le k-1)\).
Justify model: independence and uniform average rate.

Suggested lesson flow

  1. Start with the arrivals simulator: calls per minute or typos per page.
  2. Introduce \(X\sim\mathrm{Po}(\lambda)\) as a count model, not just a formula.
  3. Use the shape explorer to connect \(\lambda\), centre and spread.
  4. Practise exact, cumulative and tail probabilities with careful wording.
  5. Use the mean–variance simulator to show \(E(X)=\mathrm{Var}(X)=\lambda\).
  6. Introduce the independent sum rule through two call streams or two roads.
  7. Finish by choosing between normal, binomial and Poisson distributions in context.

Misconceptions to watch

MisconceptionIntervention

Forgetting to adjust \(\lambda\) when the interval changes.Ask “mean for what interval?” before calculating.
Confusing \(P(X\lt k)\) and \(P(X\le k)\).Use a number-line/bar-chart interpretation.
