1. Why do we need coupled differential equations?

A single differential equation such as \(\frac{dR}{dt}=kR\) assumes that the rate of change of rabbits depends only on rabbits. But many real systems have variables that change each other.

EcologyRabbits increase, foxes eat rabbits, foxes depend on rabbits for food.
DiseaseSusceptible people, infected people, and recovered people change each other.
EconomicsPrices, demand, supply, and expectations interact over time.
PhysicsPosition and velocity are linked: velocity changes position; acceleration changes velocity.
Big idea: a coupled system studies a moving point \((x(t),y(t))\). The graph in the \(xy\)-plane is not usually a graph of \(y\) against \(x\); it is the path of the whole system through time.
\[ \frac{dx}{dt}=f(x,y), \qquad \frac{dy}{dt}=g(x,y) \]

The first equation says how the horizontal coordinate changes. The second says how the vertical coordinate changes.

What should students be able to do?

  • Recognise a coupled system.
  • Interpret the meaning of each term.
  • Find equilibrium points by setting both derivatives to zero.
  • Use Euler's method numerically.
  • Draw or interpret a direction field.
  • Sketch phase trajectories.
  • Connect the model to context.
  • For linear systems, use matrix form and eigenvalues.

2. Build the predator–prey model

Let \(x\) be prey and \(y\) be predator. The classic Lotka–Volterra model is:

\[ \frac{dx}{dt}=(a-by)x, \qquad \frac{dy}{dt}=(cx-d)y \]
axPrey growth without predators. More prey means more births.
-bxyPrey loss due to predation. Encounters depend on both \(x\) and \(y\).
cxyPredator growth from available prey. More encounters mean more food.
-dyPredator natural death without food.
Modelling assumption: the term \(xy\) assumes random mixing. If prey and predators meet more often, the interaction term becomes larger.

Change one term at a time

Use the sliders to predict whether the system will cycle, spiral inward, or move toward extinction/coexistence. Then check in the phase-plane tab.

Equilibrium points

At equilibrium, the system is not changing, so both derivatives are zero.

\[ \frac{dx}{dt}=0 \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{dy}{dt}=0 \]

4. Time-series view

The phase portrait shows \(y\) against \(x\). The time-series view shows \(x(t)\) and \(y(t)\) against time.

x(t) y(t)

Important interpretation

For a predator–prey cycle, the prey usually rises first. Then predators rise because food is plentiful. Then prey falls because predation increases. Then predators fall because food is scarce. The cycle repeats.

Exam phrase: The predator population tends to lag behind the prey population because predator growth depends on the availability of prey.
Phase plane versus time graph

The phase plane does not show time on an axis. Time is hidden in the motion around the curve. The time graph makes the delay between the two populations easier to see.

5. Euler's method for a coupled system

Euler's method uses the current rates of change to make a small step forward.

\[ x_{n+1}=x_n+h f(x_n,y_n), \qquad y_{n+1}=y_n+h g(x_n,y_n) \]

Euler table

ntxydx/dtdy/dt
Accuracy warning: Euler's method follows straight-line tangent steps. A smaller step size \(h\) is usually more accurate but requires more calculations.

6. Matrix and eigenvalue connection

Some coupled systems are linear:

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} \]

Eigenvectors give special directions. If the system starts exactly on an eigenvector, it stays on that line. The corresponding eigenvalue tells whether motion grows, decays, or changes sign along that direction.

Linear system controls

Choose “Linear system” in the model tab to make these controls active.

Trace-determinant shortcut

For \(A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\), the eigenvalues are

\[ \lambda=m\pm \sqrt{m^2-p},\qquad m=\frac{a+d}{2},\quad p=ad-bc \]

Here \(m\) is half the trace and \(p\) is the determinant.

7. Exam-preparation mode

Use this section to practise the exact thinking students usually need in an exam: identify variables, interpret terms, find equilibria, use Euler's method, and describe long-term behaviour.

Command words and what they mean

CommandWhat to do
FindShow calculation and give exact or rounded value.
InterpretExplain the mathematical result in the context of the population/model.
DescribeSay what happens over time, including direction, increase/decrease, equilibrium or cycle.
Use Euler's methodWrite the update equations, substitute values carefully, repeat for required steps.
HenceUse the result from the previous part; do not start a completely new method.
High-mark answer pattern: calculation → mathematical conclusion → contextual sentence.

Checklist before an assessment

  • I can explain why an interaction term often contains \(xy\).
  • I can find equilibria by solving two equations simultaneously.
  • I can interpret \((0,0)\), extinction, coexistence, and carrying capacity.
  • I can use Euler's update formula for both variables.
  • I can read arrows in a direction field.
  • I can explain the meaning of a closed orbit or spiral.
  • I can compare phase portraits and time-series graphs.
  • I can write a linear system in matrix form.
  • I can use eigenvalues to discuss stability for a linear system.
  • I can write contextual conclusions, not just numbers.

8. Teacher mode: suggested lesson sequence

Lesson 1: intuitionStudents build the predator–prey model from words. Focus on why terms are positive, negative, and multiplied.
Lesson 2: phase planeStudents click starting points, describe trajectories, and locate equilibria.
Lesson 3: Euler methodStudents complete a table by hand, then compare with the applet.
Lesson 4: model comparisonCompare Lotka–Volterra, logistic predator–prey, and competition models.
Lesson 5: matricesUse linear systems to connect phase portraits to eigenvectors and eigenvalues.
Lesson 6: exam practiceUse mixed questions: interpretation, calculation, and long-term behaviour.
Best classroom strategy: ask students to predict first, then test in the applet, then write a sentence explaining the result mathematically and contextually.

Discussion prompts

  • Why is \((0,0)\) an equilibrium but often not the most interesting one?
  • What assumptions are hidden inside the term \(xy\)?
  • Why might the real world not follow a perfect closed cycle?
  • How could humans change the parameters in an ecosystem?
  • What does it mean if a trajectory spirals inward?
